The Irish Families Who Became Part of Spain

The extraordinary story of Irish surnames, Spanish archives and families separated by centuries

By the OneClick Spain Editorial Team · 6 min read

If you take the Madrid metro to the stop called O'Donnell, you are travelling to an Irish surname. It belongs to Leopoldo O'Donnell, Duke of Tetuán, who was prime minister of Spain in the 1850s and 1860s, and who came from an Irish family that had settled in Spain generations earlier. He is far from the only one.

A surname that crossed the sea

The O'Donnell family tell the story well. According to the family's own history, as recounted in The Irish Times, Joseph O'Donnell left Co. Mayo for Spain around the 1720s and founded the Spanish branch of the clan. About three hundred years later, Hugo O'Donnell, the seventh Duke of Tetuán, a Grandee of Spain and a former naval commander turned naval historian, became head of the O'Donnell clan in 2023. The family had been in Spain for around three centuries, and the name was still Irish.

Others who made their names in Spain

The O'Donnells were one of many. Alejandro O'Reilly, born in Ireland in 1723, became a general in the Spanish army and governor of Louisiana, which was then under Spanish rule. General Joaquín Blake commanded Spanish armies against Napoleon in the Peninsular War, and Luis de Lacy, from another Irish military family, was a leading figure in the liberal revolts of the early nineteenth century. Ricardo Wall rose to be a senior minister to the Spanish crown in the mid-eighteenth century. And on the Atlantic islands, families such as the Cologans made their fortune in the wine trade in the Canary Islands. Further afield still, Ambrosio O'Higgins, born in Co. Sligo, served the Spanish crown in South America, rising to be Viceroy of Peru, and his son Bernardo O'Higgins became the liberator of Chile.

How a surname changes

A name that crosses a border rarely stays the same. Gaelic names were written down by clerks who spelled them as they heard them. The "O'" and the "Mac" were sometimes kept, sometimes dropped, and accents and endings were added to suit Spanish. A family whose ancestors were Ó Raghallaigh might appear in a Spanish record as O'Reilly, O'Reylly, Oreilly or just Reilly. For anyone tracing a family, this is the main difficulty, and the main reason to try every spelling you can think of.

What the Spanish archives hold

The good news is that Spain kept very good records. The Archivo General de Simancas, the old royal archive near Valladolid, holds the Spanish state papers of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, including documents on Ireland. Spanish military records, held in other national archives, follow Irish officers through their careers. Irish officers who wanted commissions often had to document their noble descent, which means the files can contain family trees that go back to Ireland. Many of these records can be searched free through PARES, the Spanish state's online archives portal. And the records of the Irish College at Salamanca, taken to Maynooth in 1952, document the Irish students who studied there.

Separated by centuries

The result is one of the odd features of this story. There are Spanish families with Irish names who have kept their family papers for centuries, and Irish families who never knew that a branch of their name went to Spain. Some of the links are being rebuilt: Lifford in Donegal and Simancas in Spain are planning to twin their towns, and Valladolid has hosted gatherings of Spanish-Irish associations. Many more connections have not yet been found.
