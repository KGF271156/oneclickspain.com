The Irish Families Who Became Part of Spain

The extraordinary story of Irish surnames, Spanish archives and families separated by centuries

By the OneClick Spain Editorial Team · 6 min read

Imagine discovering that your Spanish surname was once an Irish name.

Perhaps your ancestors came from Cork, Kerry, Donegal or Ulster. Perhaps they arrived in Spain more than 300 years ago, became part of Spanish society and gradually adapted their names to a new language.

It sounds like something from a historical novel, but it is supported by remarkable archival research.

The relationship between Ireland and Spain is centuries old. Irish soldiers, merchants, priests, students and aristocratic families travelled to Spain in considerable numbers, particularly during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.

Some became Spanish military officers, others established businesses, and many married and raised families.

Over time, some of their Irish surnames changed so dramatically that their original identity became difficult to recognise.

One Irish historian devoted much of her career to uncovering these connections.

Her name was Micheline Kerney Walsh.

The Wild Geese arrive in Spain

The story begins with a period of considerable upheaval in Ireland.

During the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, English expansion and religious restrictions placed enormous pressure on Ireland's Catholic aristocracy.

The defeat of Irish forces at Kinsale in 1601 and the subsequent Flight of the Earls in 1607 contributed to a wave of Irish emigration.

Many Irish soldiers entered the service of European Catholic powers, particularly Spain and France.

They became known as the Wild Geese.

But Spain offered more than military employment.

Irish families could establish themselves in Spanish towns and cities, educate their children, practise their religion and develop new commercial and social connections.

The Irish colleges in Salamanca and elsewhere helped maintain these relationships, while Irish merchants established themselves in important trading centres.

The result was a network of Irish families spread across Spain.

The extraordinary work of Micheline Kerney Walsh

One of the most important historians to investigate these families was Micheline Kerney Walsh.

Between 1960 and 1978, she published four volumes entitled Spanish Knights of Irish Origin: Documents from Continental Archives.

The books were published by the Irish Manuscripts Commission and are now available in digital form.

Her work was based on original documents preserved in continental archives, particularly the Spanish historical collections.

She investigated Irishmen who had been admitted to Spain's prestigious military orders of chivalry.

These included the Orders of Santiago, Calatrava, Alcántara and Montesa.

Admission was not simply a military honour. Applicants had to establish their ancestry and provide evidence of their family background.

The surviving documentation included family genealogies, baptismal records, marriage information, wills and statements from witnesses.

In Volume One, Walsh documented 60 Irish-origin knights of the Order of Santiago, together with more than 800 Irish-origin individuals who acted as sponsors.

The documents reveal a surprisingly extensive Irish presence within Spanish society.

When Gallagher became Galchurio

Perhaps the most intriguing aspect of Walsh's research is the transformation of Irish surnames.

When Irish emigrants arrived in Spain, their names were sometimes written down according to how they sounded to Spanish-speaking clerks.

In other cases, families adopted Spanish equivalents or used existing Spanish names that resembled their original Irish surnames.

The results can be astonishing.

Gallagher appears as Galchurio.

Wadding becomes Guadin.

Conway becomes Combeo.

Fitzgerald becomes Gemiamo.

Nangle becomes Ángulo.

Keegan becomes Quegano.

Hurley becomes Urleo.

Darcy becomes de Arseo.

Lynch becomes Lince.

These examples appear in the introduction to Volume One of Walsh's research.

She also explains that Irish first names were frequently Hispanicised.

Edward could become Duarte or Eduardo, James could become Diego, Santiago or Jaime, and Eoghan could become Eugenio.

Sometimes the surname itself became a Spanish translation.

For example, Woulfe could be recorded as Lobo, meaning wolf, and White as Blanco.

In other cases, the Irish connection became almost impossible to recognise because an emigrant adopted a common Spanish surname.

Walsh's research is particularly important because the documentary evidence allows historians to connect names that might otherwise appear unrelated.

The military connection

Irishmen were not simply visitors to Spain. Many established long military careers.

Irish regiments became an important feature of the Spanish military establishment.

The Regimiento de Irlanda, Regimiento de Hibernia and Regimiento de Ultonia all reflected the long-standing Irish military presence.

Some Irish officers became senior commanders and developed reputations within the Spanish army.

Their families could acquire status and recognition, and subsequent generations increasingly regarded Spain as home.

For anyone researching an Irish ancestor who served in the Spanish military, Spanish service records and genealogical documents can provide valuable clues.

The Irish colleges and the preservation of family connections

Religion and education were also important.

Irish colleges in Spain provided a home for students and clergy who could no longer pursue their education freely in Ireland.

Salamanca became particularly significant.

These institutions helped maintain a sense of Irish identity abroad while also integrating Irish students into Spanish intellectual and religious life.

Some families maintained their Irish connections for generations, even when their descendants were speaking Spanish as their first language.

It is a reminder that migration does not necessarily mean the loss of identity. Sometimes it creates a new one.

Did the relationship work in the opposite direction?

The movement of people and names was not entirely one-way.

Spanish merchants, sailors and other individuals also travelled to Ireland.

Galway is particularly interesting because of its historic international trading connections. Its merchants traded with Spain and other parts of continental Europe.

Cork and Waterford also developed important commercial relationships.

Some Spanish individuals settled in Ireland, married locally and became part of Irish communities.

However, the scale of this movement was considerably smaller than the Irish migration to Spain.

We should also distinguish genuine Spanish family origins from surnames that merely sound Spanish.

For example, not every Irish family with a Spanish-looking surname has Spanish ancestry, and the Armada folklore should not be treated as proof of a family's origins.

Individual parish records, marriage certificates and genealogical evidence are essential.

The Armada: a real event surrounded by legend

The Spanish Armada of 1588 left another enduring mark on Irish history.

Several Spanish ships were wrecked along the western coast of Ireland, and survivors encountered local communities.

Captain Francisco de Cuéllar's account of his experiences remains one of the most fascinating personal records of the period.

There are popular stories about Spanish sailors settling in Ireland and contributing to the darker colouring sometimes associated with the Black Irish.

There are also legends about dogs surviving Armada shipwrecks and influencing the development of Irish terrier breeds.

These stories are part of the shared folklore of Ireland and Spain, although the evidence does not establish them as explanations for Irish surnames or the origins of those dog breeds.

The Armada is one chapter in the relationship, but the longer history of Irish settlement in Spain provides much stronger evidence of lasting family connections.

A new generation of Irish-Spanish families

Today, the relationship between the two countries is continuing in a different way.

Irish and Spanish people meet through work, travel, education, business and marriage.

Modern families increasingly combine surnames from different national backgrounds.

An Irish-Spanish child might inherit an Irish surname from one parent and a Spanish surname from the other, preserving both family histories.

This is a very different process from the seventeenth-century Hispanicisation of Irish names, but it represents another stage in the same long story.

And perhaps it will be future historians who investigate today's Irish-Spanish families to discover how our names and identities have evolved.

Could your Spanish surname have Irish origins?

The work of Micheline Kerney Walsh gives us an extraordinary starting point.

Her books demonstrate that Irish families became established in Spain and that their descendants can sometimes be traced through documents preserved for centuries.

Perhaps a Spanish reader will recognise one of the transformed surnames.

Perhaps an Irish family will discover that an ancestor travelled to Spain and never returned.

We would love to hear from readers who have discovered an Irish connection in their Spanish family history.

Two countries, centuries of shared history, and countless family stories still waiting to be discovered.
