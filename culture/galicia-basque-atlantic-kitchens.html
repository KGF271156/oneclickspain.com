Galicia and the Basque Country: The Atlantic Kitchens of Spain

Octopus, caldo galego, marmitako and the Basque fishermen who sailed to Irish waters

By the OneClick Spain Editorial Team · 13 min read

If you have read Part One on OneClickIreland, you will know that the kitchens of western Ireland and north-west Spain have more in common than most people expect. Here we look at the Spanish side in more detail: the sea-and-land cooking of Galicia, the fishermen's food of the Basque Country, and a piece of history that links the Basque coast to the west of Ireland.

We also have four recipes to try at home, and an invitation to share your own.

Galicia: a cuisine of sea and land

Galicia is not simply a seafood cuisine. Its food draws on two worlds: the ocean on the coast and the farms of the interior.

On the coast, the rías and the open Atlantic supply octopus, mussels, clams, cockles, scallops, crab, hake, sardines, turbot and percebes (goose barnacles), which are gathered from wave-battered rocks and are among the most prized and expensive shellfish in Spain.

Inland, the picture changes to green pastures, vegetable gardens and smallholdings. The traditional Galician larder includes potatoes, white beans, cabbage and turnip greens, pork and beef, cheese, bread and maize flour. Many of the most characteristic dishes, from caldo galego to lacón con grelos, belong to this farmhouse side of the cuisine.

The approach is the same in both: good ingredients, simple cooking, and food that feeds a family.

Polbo á feira: the octopus that became a symbol

No dish says "Galicia" quite like polbo á feira. The octopus is boiled in a copper pot, cut into rounds with scissors, and served on a wooden plate with olive oil, coarse salt and paprika, usually over slices of boiled potato.

Its name comes from the feira, the fair, where the pulpeiras cook the octopus in large pots and serve it to the crowds. In towns such as Carballiño and Melide, an octopus fair is as much a social occasion as a meal. People eat at long tables, share plates and drink the local wine from white ceramic bowls called cuncas.

The technique is the secret. Traditionally the octopus is lowered into the boiling water and lifted out three times before being left to cook, a ritual called asustar el pulpo, "frightening the octopus", which is said to stop the skin from breaking and help the tentacles curl. Octopus is often frozen before cooking, which also helps to tenderise it.

Caldo galego, lacón con grelos and the farmhouse pot

If octopus is Galicia at the fair, caldo galego is Galicia at home. This is the region's traditional broth, made with white beans, potatoes, greens and a little pork, often with unto (cured pork fat) for flavour. The greens may be cabbage or grelos, the leafy tops of turnips, which are a Galician signature.

It is the kind of dish that could have been cooked in a farmhouse in Kerry or Mayo. Nourishing, economical and made from what the land produces, it is a pot meal in the truest sense.

Its richer relative is lacón con grelos, in which cured pork shoulder is boiled with turnip greens, potatoes and often chorizo. It is closely associated with the carnival season (Entroido) in late winter, when Galician families sit down to the dish before the austerity of Lent. An Irish reader will not need the comparison with bacon and cabbage pointed out.

A taste of each Galician province

Galicia has four provinces, and each has its own food identity.

A Coruña takes in Santiago de Compostela, where the Camino ends and the almond tarta de Santiago is the pilgrim's sweet. It is also home to the small green Padrón peppers, fried in olive oil and salt, of which the old saying goes that "some are hot and some are not", the famous tortilla of Betanzos, and the Arzúa-Ulloa cheeses. Octopus is the speciality at Melide.

Lugo is the heart of Galician beef country, with green pastures, chestnuts and the steep vineyards of the Ribeira Sacra, where wine is grown on terraces above the river canyons of the Sil.

Ourense is inland, famous for the octopus fair at O Carballiño and for the Ribeiro and Valdeorras wines, as well as its thermal springs.

Pontevedra is the shellfish capital. It gives its name to the Rías Baixas, where Albariño wine is made at Cambados and mussels are raised on floating rafts in the estuaries. The Festa do Marisco at O Grove, the oysters of the ría de Vigo and the big winter pork-and-vegetable cocido feast of Lalín all belong here.

Empanada: Galicia's pie

Galicia's other great portable dish is the empanada. A Galician empanada is a large, flat pie baked in a tray and cut into squares, with a thin, bread-like or olive-oil pastry and a filling that varies by town and family. Tuna, sardines, cod with raisins, cockles, pork loin, chicken and meat-and-vegetable fillings are all common, usually bound with a slow-cooked sofrito of onion, peppers and tomato.

Empanadas travel well, which is exactly why they are so popular at picnics, fiestas and on pilgrim routes. They are sold at the fairs, baked at home for celebrations and wrapped to take on a journey. The Irish equivalent would be a good meat or fish pie or a pasty in the pocket of a farm worker: a hot, sealed meal that needs no plate.

The Basque Country: where the fishermen cooked aboard

Moving east along the Spanish Atlantic coast brings us to the Basque Country, where the sea is just as important and the food culture is internationally famous.

Basque cooking is known for its pintxos, small bites served on bread or on skewers in bars, for grilled fish and beef cooked over charcoal, and for a deep respect for the ingredient. Salt cod, bacalao, appears in many classic recipes, notably bacalao al pil-pil, where the cod is slowly cooked in olive oil and garlic until the natural gelatine forms an emulsified sauce, and bacalao a la vizcaína, with a sauce of dried red peppers.

The dish most closely associated with the Basque fishing tradition, though, is marmitako. The name comes from marmita, the cooking pot, and the stew of tuna, potatoes, peppers and onion is traditionally said to have been made by fishermen on board their boats. Potatoes extend the fish and make a full meal. Anyone who has made a seafood stew on a boat, or been fed one by a fisherman's family on the Irish coast, will recognise the idea.

Pintxos, cider houses and eating as a social act

The Basque tradition has a different rhythm from the Galician one but the same social heart. In San Sebastián and the other Basque towns, people move from bar to bar, taking a pintxo and a small glass of wine or txakoli at each. The food is a reason to meet and to talk.

The Basque cider house, or sidrería, is another institution. In the cider season, diners sit at long tables, and when the barrels are opened they take their glasses to catch the jet of cider. The traditional menu is a ritual of its own: salt-cod omelette, cod with peppers, a thick grilled steak, and cheese with quince paste and walnuts. A visitor from Ireland might think of the long table at a wedding or a hooley, where the food and the company are one and the same.

The Basque boats off the Irish coast

There is a historical thread behind all this that is more than coincidence, and it is well documented.

Basque fishermen were sailing to Irish waters centuries ago. According to research by the historian Selma Barkham, writing in History Ireland, Basque mariners were fishing off the south-western and western coasts of Ireland by the early sixteenth century, and in a lawsuit of 1506 witnesses testified that fishermen from San Sebastián "have been accustomed to go fishing [in Ireland] from times immemorial". They fished largely for hake, conger eel and herring, as well as pilchards, and also hunted whales.

Records show that the fleets sailed each summer, with crews working around the Aran Islands off Galway and aiming to leave Ireland by Christmas to sell their catch in Spanish and Italian markets. In the later Middle Ages, Castilian merchants and fishermen negotiated with Irish lords for permission to fish, offering hides, wine, salt, iron and textiles in return.

For a time, therefore, the waters off the west of Ireland were a regular workplace for Spanish seamen. The sea did not separate Ireland and Spain. It connected their fishing communities, ports and merchants.

It would be an overreach to claim that these visits shaped particular Irish recipes. We have no evidence of that, and we should not invent it. What we can say is that centuries of contact between Atlantic communities provide a rich historical backdrop to the family resemblances in their food.

What the kitchens share

Step back and the pattern is clear. Ireland, Galicia and the Basque Country share:

  • A respect for local, seasonal ingredients, especially seafood.
  • Potatoes as a staple that stretches a meal.
  • A one-pot tradition of soups and stews.
  • Pork, cabbage and greens as the backbone of the farmhouse kitchen.
  • Pastry and bread as portable food.
  • A culture in which the table is where a community comes together.

The flavours are different. Olive oil, paprika and garlic here; butter, milk and herbs there. But the instinct is the same.

Four recipes to try at home

Here are four traditional dishes from the Spanish side. Cook them alongside the Irish chowder and colcannon from Part One, and see what you think.

Polbo á feira (Galician-style octopus)

Serves 4 · Preparation 15 minutes · Cooking about 45–60 minutes

Ingredients

  • 1 octopus, about 1.5–2kg, cleaned and thawed if frozen
  • 4 medium potatoes
  • Extra-virgin olive oil
  • Sweet paprika, and a little hot paprika if you like
  • Coarse sea salt

Method

  1. Bring a large pot of water to the boil.
  2. Traditionally, dip the octopus into the boiling water and lift it out three times before submerging it fully. This helps the tentacles curl.
  3. Cook until tender. Start checking after about 40 minutes: a knife should slide easily into the thickest part.
  4. Remove the octopus and let it rest briefly.
  5. Cook the potatoes in the octopus cooking water until tender, then slice them.
  6. Cut the octopus into rounds with scissors.
  7. Arrange the octopus over the potatoes on a wooden plate. Drizzle generously with olive oil, sprinkle with paprika and finish with coarse salt.

Caldo galego

Serves 4–6 · Preparation 20 minutes · Cooking about 40 minutes (plus soaking time for dried beans)

Ingredients

  • 250g dried white beans, soaked overnight, or 1 large tin
  • 3 potatoes, diced
  • 250g cabbage or turnip greens
  • 1 onion
  • 2 cloves garlic
  • 100g smoked bacon or pork belly
  • 1 chorizo (optional)
  • 1 litre stock or water
  • 2 tablespoons olive oil
  • Salt and pepper

Method

  1. If using dried beans, cook them until nearly tender.
  2. Heat the olive oil in a large saucepan and gently fry the onion and garlic.
  3. Add the bacon and chorizo, if using.
  4. Add the potatoes and stock and simmer for about 15 minutes.
  5. Add the beans and greens and cook for another 15–20 minutes until everything is tender.
  6. Season to taste. Some families mash a few potatoes or beans into the broth to give it body.

Marmitako (Basque tuna and potato stew)

Serves 4 · Preparation 20 minutes · Cooking about 35 minutes

Ingredients

  • 500g fresh tuna, cut into chunks
  • 600g potatoes
  • 1 onion
  • 1 green pepper and 1 red pepper
  • 2 cloves garlic
  • 2 ripe tomatoes, chopped
  • 1 teaspoon sweet paprika
  • 750ml fish stock
  • Olive oil and salt
  • Fresh parsley

Method

  1. Heat olive oil in a large saucepan and cook the chopped onion and peppers gently for 10 minutes.
  2. Add the garlic and tomatoes and cook until softened.
  3. Stir in the paprika briefly.
  4. Add the potatoes, traditionally broken rather than cut cleanly so they release starch and thicken the stew, then pour in the fish stock.
  5. Simmer for 20–25 minutes until the potatoes are tender.
  6. Add the tuna and cook gently for 3–5 minutes, depending on the size of the chunks.
  7. Finish with parsley and serve immediately.

Galician tuna empanada

Makes 1 large pie, serving 6–8 · Preparation 1 hour (including resting) · Baking 35–45 minutes

Pastry

  • 500g plain flour
  • 150ml olive oil
  • 150ml warm water
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon paprika

Filling

  • 2 onions, finely sliced
  • 1 red pepper
  • 2 tins of tuna, drained
  • 2 tomatoes, chopped
  • 2 tablespoons olive oil
  • 1 teaspoon paprika
  • 2 hard-boiled eggs, chopped (optional)
  • 1 egg, beaten, to glaze

Method

  1. Mix the flour, salt and paprika. Add the oil and warm water and bring together into a dough. Knead briefly and rest for 30 minutes.
  2. Heat the oil and cook the onions and pepper slowly until soft.
  3. Add the tomatoes and paprika and cook until the mixture has reduced.
  4. Stir in the tuna and the chopped eggs, if using. Allow to cool.
  5. Divide the pastry in two. Roll out one half and place it on a baking tray.
  6. Spread the filling over it, cover with the second half and seal the edges.
  7. Make a small hole in the top for steam and brush with beaten egg.
  8. Bake at 190°C (170°C fan) for 35–45 minutes until golden.
