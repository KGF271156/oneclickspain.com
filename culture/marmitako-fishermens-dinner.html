The Fishermen’s Dinner: Marmitako and the Irish Coast

In the Basque Country, fishermen cooked marmitako on board. In Ireland, coastal families made chowder. One Atlantic Ocean.

By the OneClick Spain Editorial Team · 3 min read

In the Basque Country, fishermen created marmitako, a hearty stew of tuna, potatoes and peppers, cooked in a pot on board their boats.

In Ireland, generations of coastal families have made their own fish stews and chowders from whatever the boats landed that day.

Two countries. Two traditions. One Atlantic Ocean.

The pot on the boat

The name marmitako comes from marmita, the Basque word for the cooking pot. The story goes that crews fishing for tuna out in the Bay of Biscay needed a hot meal that could be made with a few things from the boat's stores: potatoes, onions, peppers, olive oil and the fish itself.

The method was simple. Onions and peppers were softened in oil, potatoes broken into the pot to thicken the stew, water or stock added, and the tuna dropped in at the very end so that it stayed tender. Anyone who has cooked a meal on a boat will understand the appeal: one pot, no waste, plenty of calories after a long day in the wind.

The Irish equivalent

In Ireland, the nearest equivalents are the fish chowders and stews of the western coast: a pot of potatoes, onions and fish, finished with milk or cream. They also grew from the practicalities of life by the sea, where the catch came in daily and the potato supply was close at hand.

The seasonings differ. Where marmitako uses paprika, peppers and tomato, an Irish chowder uses butter, milk, leeks and bacon. But the principle is shared: use the day's fish, stretch it with potatoes and feed everyone.

A shared sea

The link between these two traditions is more than a matter of similar cooking. As we explain in our Part Two feature, Basque fishing crews were sailing to Irish waters by the early sixteenth century. Historical research shows that they fished off the south-west and west of Ireland for hake, conger eel and herring, and worked around the Aran Islands off Galway. They were regular visitors to Irish waters for generations.

We should be careful here. There is no evidence that those fishermen taught Irish cooks their recipes, and we don't claim they did. But the picture of Basque crews and Irish coastal communities sharing the same waters, sometimes trading with each other, gives a richer background to the family resemblance in their food.

Marmitako at home

Marmitako is one of the easiest dishes in this series to make, and it works well with fresh tuna, though other firm fish can be used. The full recipe is in Part Two. It takes about 35 minutes from start to finish.
