The Music of Galicia

The Gaita, the Muiñeira and the Surprising Connection with Ireland and Scotland

Discover the extraordinary folk music of north-west Spain – where bagpipes, lively dances, haunting songs and centuries of community tradition create a sound that may feel surprisingly familiar to Irish and Scottish ears.

By the OneClick Spain Editorial Team · 11 min read · 5 videos

When many people think of Spanish music, they imagine flamenco: the guitar, the handclaps, the dancing and the passionate rhythms of Andalucía.

But travel to the far north-west of Spain, to Galicia, and you discover a very different musical world.

Here, the sound of the bagpipe can fill a village square. Fiddles and flutes lead traditional dances. Women sing old songs while accompanying themselves on tambourines. Music is played at festivals, religious celebrations, family gatherings and informal community sessions.

For visitors from Ireland or Scotland, there can be a moment of recognition. The instruments seem familiar. The dance rhythms have a lively, lilting quality. The sound of the pipes carries across the landscape in a way that might bring to mind a Highland gathering or an Irish traditional music session.

Yet Galician music is not an imitation of Irish or Scottish music. It is a rich and distinctive tradition with its own history, language, repertoire and regional character.

So what makes Galician music special? And why have musicians from Galicia, Ireland and Scotland found so much common ground?

Galicia: a different musical Spain

Galicia occupies the north-west corner of the Iberian Peninsula. It faces the Atlantic and shares a long coastline of fishing villages, estuaries, ports and rugged headlands.

It is also one of Spain's most distinctive cultural regions. Galician, a language closely related to Portuguese, is spoken alongside Spanish, and local traditions remain an important part of regional identity.

Music has long been central to community life. It accompanies dancing, religious celebrations, processions, seasonal events and gatherings where neighbours come together.

In December 2025, the Galician government, the Xunta de Galicia, formally declared Galicia's traditional music and dance a Ben de Interese Cultural (an asset of cultural interest) in the category of intangible heritage. The declaration was published in Spain's official state gazette in February 2026. It recognised the importance of music and dance as living traditions, transmitted and renewed across generations.

That recognition is significant. It acknowledges that traditional music is not simply something performed for tourists or preserved in museums. It is a living part of Galician identity.

The gaita: the unmistakable voice of Galicia

The first instrument to discover is the gaita.

The Galician gaita is a bagpipe, and its sound is one of the most recognisable features of the region's traditional music.

Like other bagpipes, it uses a bag to maintain a continuous supply of air to the melody pipe. The familiar Galician form is generally mouth-blown: the player fills the bag through a blowpipe and controls the airflow with pressure from the arm.

The instrument commonly has a chanter, on which the melody is played, and one or more drones that produce sustained notes beneath it.

There are different types and historical variations of the gaita across Galicia. Instruments have changed over time, and modern makers and performers continue to explore their possibilities.

For an Irish listener, the sound may immediately suggest the uilleann pipes. For a Scottish listener, it may bring to mind the Highland bagpipe. But the gaita has its own tone and character. It can sound bright, powerful and celebratory, especially when played outdoors with drums and other instruments.

It is heard at festivals, processions, dances and public celebrations. In many places, a piper is not simply a musician hired to entertain: the instrument is part of the community's sense of occasion and identity.

The muiñeira: the dance that brings Galicia to life

If there is one Galician dance form that visitors should discover, it is the muiñeira.

The muiñeira is commonly associated with a lively 6/8 rhythm. It has a skipping, turning quality that may remind an Irish listener of a jig.

That resemblance is worth exploring, but the muiñeira is not simply a Spanish jig. It has its own melodic patterns, accents, phrasing and dance movements. Different parts of Galicia have their own variations, and the music can be played with considerable energy and virtuosity.

The muiñeira is often accompanied by the gaita, but other instruments may join in, including fiddle, flute and percussion.

The dance itself is an important part of the experience. Traditional Galician dancing can involve couples and groups, with steps and movements that are distinctive to the region. The music is not just a soundtrack: it shapes the movement and brings the dancers together.

There is also the jota, another lively dance form with many regional variations. Alongside these are alboradas, alalás, processional tunes, work songs and sung romances.

The result is a musical culture far richer than the image of a bagpiper playing a single kind of tune.

The alalá: a quieter, more haunting Galicia

Not all Galician music is fast and energetic.

The alalá is a lyrical form of traditional song, often with a freer, more reflective character. It can be haunting and deeply expressive, with the human voice taking centre stage.

For an Irish reader, it may bring to mind a slow air or a traditional song sung without elaborate accompaniment. For a Scottish listener, there may be echoes of the emotional quality found in older Gaelic song traditions.

These are comparisons of feeling rather than proof of a direct musical origin. Each tradition has its own language, poetic conventions and musical style.

But the emotional connection is easy to understand. Folk music is not only for dancing. It carries stories of love, loss, work, family, humour and the passing of time.

The women who carried the songs

One of the most important parts of Galician music is the contribution of women.

The cantareiras were traditional female singers who preserved and transmitted songs through family and community life. The pandereteiras accompanied singing with the pandeireta, a hand-held percussion instrument similar in appearance to a tambourine.

These women sang at gatherings, during agricultural work, at social occasions and in the home. Their repertoire included love songs, humorous verses, social commentary, work songs and traditional poetry.

The pandeireta is not simply a device for keeping time. In skilled hands it provides a distinctive rhythmic language, with techniques and patterns that are particular to Galician performance.

The importance of these women is now receiving greater recognition. They were not merely keeping old songs alive; they were creating, adapting and transmitting a living musical culture.

This is a particularly interesting comparison with Ireland and Scotland, where women have also played a vital role in preserving songs and passing musical knowledge through families and communities.

The history of folk music is often told through famous pipers, fiddlers and composers. But much of its survival has depended on people whose names were never written into the history books.

More than the bagpipes: Galicia's musical instruments

The gaita may be the best-known Galician instrument, but it is far from the only one.

The pandeireta is a hand percussion instrument used particularly in traditional singing. It is closely associated with the cantareiras and pandereteiras.

The pandeiro is a square frame drum used in some regional traditions.

The tambor and bombo provide rhythmic support for gaita bands and dance music.

The fiddle is part of the wider instrumental tradition and can be heard in both traditional and contemporary arrangements.

The requinta is a wooden transverse flute used in Galician music.

The zanfoña, or hurdy-gurdy, is a fascinating string instrument played by turning a wheel with a handle while pressing keys to change the notes. It belongs to a wider European family of instruments and has a place in Galician traditional music.

This variety makes Galicia particularly interesting for anyone who enjoys comparing European folk traditions. It is not just a story about one instrument shared by several countries. It is a musical culture with a whole range of sounds and practices.

What does Galicia have in common with Ireland?

The similarities between Galician and Irish music are often immediately apparent to listeners.

Both traditions feature bagpipes, fiddle, flute and dance music. Both have lively compound-time rhythms. Both include music that is learned by ear and passed on through playing and participation. And both have strong links between music, community celebrations and local identity.

The muiñeira and Irish jig provide an obvious point of comparison. Their rhythms can feel related, even though their musical structures and dance traditions are distinct.

The gaita and uilleann pipes provide another fascinating comparison. Both use a bag to sustain the melody, but the familiar Galician instrument is generally mouth-blown, while the Irish pipes use bellows. Their sounds and playing techniques are different.

There are also differences in the wider musical culture. Irish traditional sessions are often centred on groups of musicians playing tunes together, sometimes informally in pubs or homes. Galician music has its own social settings, including the foliada, where singing, dancing and instrumental music come together in a participatory gathering.

Both traditions are living, social and continually evolving.

And what about Scotland?

Scotland is another important part of this musical conversation.

The Scottish tradition has its own powerful bagpipe identity, particularly through the Great Highland bagpipe, as well as fiddle music and the dance forms of reels, jigs and strathspeys.

The strathspey is especially distinctive. Its dotted rhythms and characteristic Scotch snap give it a different feel from the more flowing reel or the lilting jig.

A Scottish listener hearing a Galician muiñeira may recognise a familiar dance pulse, but the tune will not necessarily behave like a Scottish jig. The phrasing, accents and steps are different.

That is part of the pleasure of listening across traditions. Similarities invite us in, while differences give us something new to discover.

There are also modern musical connections between Galicia and Scotland, particularly through festivals, international piping events and the wider Celtic music movement. Musicians have increasingly met on stages where the different bagpipe traditions can be heard alongside one another.

The Celtic question: is Galicia a Celtic country?

Galicia is frequently described as one of Europe's Celtic regions. The idea is important to many aspects of Galician cultural identity, particularly in music, festivals and regional heritage.

The bagpipe, the Atlantic landscape and the region's cultural connections with Ireland, Scotland, Wales and Brittany all contribute to that identity.

But the word 'Celtic' needs some care.

It can refer to ancient peoples and languages, to modern cultural identities, or to a contemporary musical category. Those meanings are not necessarily the same.

The existence of bagpipes or a lively 6/8 dance rhythm does not, by itself, prove that a musical form has survived unchanged from ancient Celtic times.

Galician music also shares characteristics with neighbouring traditions in Asturias, Portugal and León, as well as the wider musical history of western Europe. Over time it has incorporated and adapted forms such as waltzes, polkas, mazurkas and other dance styles.

The best way to understand Galicia is to appreciate its own history and musical identity, rather than trying to fit it into someone else's.

When Galicia met Ireland: Carlos Núñez and The Chieftains

One of the most exciting chapters in the modern story is the relationship between Galician piper Carlos Núñez and the Irish group The Chieftains.

Núñez grew up in Vigo and became fascinated by the music of Ireland and Scotland. He met The Chieftains when they visited Galicia, and the encounter helped develop a musical relationship that would introduce Galician music to audiences around the world.

The collaboration culminated in part in the album Santiago, released in 1996.

The project explored Galician music and its connections with other Atlantic and international traditions. It included performances with Carlos Núñez and the Galician group Milladoiro, alongside musicians from other musical backgrounds.

One track is particularly enjoyable for anyone interested in the relationship between the pipes: 'Dueling Chanters'. It brings the Irish uilleann pipes and Galician gaita together, allowing their different voices to answer and challenge one another.

The album won the Grammy for Traditional World Music Album in 1997.

It is a wonderful example of what happens when musicians approach another tradition with curiosity and respect. The result is not a compromise in which one sound disappears into the other. Instead, each instrument retains its character while creating something new.

Milladoiro: a group that helped take Galician music to the world

Another important name is Milladoiro, one of Galicia's best-known folk groups.

Formed in the late 1970s, Milladoiro helped bring Galician traditional music to wider audiences. Their work draws on regional repertoire while exploring arrangements and instrumentation that connect Galicia with other European folk traditions.

Their album A Galicia de Maeloc is a useful starting point for anyone interested in the development of modern Galician folk music.

Groups such as Milladoiro helped demonstrate that traditional music need not be frozen in time. It can remain rooted in local identity while being performed in concert halls, recorded for international audiences and developed through collaboration.

A listening guide: discover the sound of Galicia

If you are new to Galician music, here are some ways to begin.

1. Start with a traditional muiñeira.

Listen for the lively 6/8 rhythm and the relationship between the melody and the dance. Then compare it with an Irish jig. The similarities are enjoyable, but pay attention to the different phrasing.

2. Listen to a gaita performance.

Notice the continuous drone and the bright sound of the chanter. Compare it with an Irish uilleann pipe recording and a Scottish Highland pipe performance.

3. Find a performance by cantareiras or pandereteiras.

This is a chance to hear the importance of women's voices and hand percussion in Galician music.

4. Explore Carlos Núñez.

His recordings and performances offer an accessible introduction to the gaita and to collaborations with musicians from other traditions.

5. Listen to Milladoiro.

Their music provides a wider picture of Galician instrumental folk music and its development.

6. Finish with The Chieftains' Santiago.

Try 'Dueling Chanters' and listen for the conversation between the Irish and Galician pipes. It is a fitting ending to a musical journey that began in Galicia and travelled across the Atlantic.

A tradition that belongs to Galicia – and welcomes the world

Galician folk music has a distinctive identity. Its gaita, muiñeira, alalás, pandeiretas, cantareiras and foliadas are not simply variations on Irish or Scottish traditions. They belong to a region with its own language, history and cultural life.

Yet the music also demonstrates how much can be gained when traditions meet.

Irish musicians have found familiar rhythms and instruments in Galicia. Scottish listeners may recognise the energy of the dance music while discovering something quite different in the detail. Galician musicians have brought their own sound to international stages, collaborating with artists from across Europe and beyond.

Perhaps that is the real connection between these Atlantic musical cultures. They are rooted in their own communities, but they are not closed to the world.
